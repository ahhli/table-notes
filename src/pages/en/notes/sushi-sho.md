Eating Japanese food in Stockholm raises an unavoidable question: should the measure be Tokyo, or should the restaurant be allowed to make sushi that belongs specifically to the Nordic world?

My dinner at Sushi Sho moved back and forth between those two ideas. During the opening courses, I wondered whether the kitchen was too eager to invent. Once the nigiri began, the meal slowly recovered the rhythm and discipline I had been waiting for.

> When the kitchen tried to design too much, the details often drifted. When Nordic ingredients entered a sound Edomae framework, the restaurant finally gave me a reason for its existence in Stockholm.

<h2 id="chawanmushi">Chawanmushi: beautiful heat control, unconvincing dashi</h2>

The first course was chawanmushi. Technically, it was finely steamed: a smooth surface, no visible air holes and no water separating from the custard. The egg-to-liquid ratio, filtering and temperature all appeared to be under control.

But tenderness is only the baseline. Chawanmushi is, at heart, dashi suspended inside barely set egg. Heat determines the texture; the broth gives it a soul.

That was where this bowl lost me. The sauce on top tasted oddly calibrated, and the dashi lacked the clarity, softness and depth I wanted. It wore a beautiful surface, but the first spoonful did not establish trust in the menu ahead.

<h2 id="turbot">Turbot fish ball: the original texture was lost, and no new one replaced it</h2>

The second course appeared to use turbot. Its white flesh had been formed into a fish ball, served in a clear soup and lifted with finely cut spring onion. The idea was easy to understand: white fish, clear broth and a clean green fragrance.

The problem was texture. The fish ball felt loose and mealy, without the elasticity or cohesion good fish paste should possess. Turbot naturally offers firm muscle fibres and a measure of gelatinous richness. Breaking it down is not inherently wrong, but once that natural structure is removed, the kitchen has to build a new one.

This course landed in an awkward middle: **the fish had lost its native texture, while the fish ball had failed to acquire a convincing texture of its own.**

<figure class="story-photo story-photo--wide"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/02-turbot-fishball.jpg" alt="Turbot fish ball with finely sliced spring onion in clear broth" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><span>02</span> The broth and spring onion were restrained; the loose texture of the fish ball became the focus.</figcaption></figure>

<h2 id="inventions">The early inventions: plenty of ideas, not enough precision</h2>

A sashimi course of hirame, hiramasa and chutoro followed. Nothing was notably wrong, yet little stayed with me. The next dish—chopped akami under a snowfall of freshly grated horseradish—finally declared a Nordic identity.

Fresh horseradish, pickled daikon and lean tuna made conceptual sense. This was not Tokyo cosplay; it brought a familiar Northern European pungency into a Japanese structure. Yet the tuna carried a distinct bloody acidity. If that note remained visible beneath such forceful condiments, the central ingredient had not quite supported the idea built around it.

<figure class="story-pair"><div><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/03-kirimi-egg-yolk-okra.jpg" alt="An opening dish of egg yolk, okra and diced tuna" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/04-akami-horseradish.jpg" alt="Chopped akami covered with a generous snowfall of freshly grated horseradish" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></div><figcaption><span>03—04</span> The opening courses were eager to invent: egg yolk, okra and tuna; then akami beneath freshly grated horseradish.</figcaption></figure>

The Tamago Kake Gohan had a similar imbalance. The salmon was well judged, and crisp, smoky fish skin provided welcome contrast. But the egg seemed to have been mixed into the rice too early, leaving the grains wet and indistinct. A heavy hand with sushi vinegar and citrus then shifted the entire bowl toward acidity.

The chef clearly had ideas and knew he was cooking in Stockholm. The question was precision—especially the calibration of acid and texture.

<figure class="story-photo story-photo--portrait"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/05-tkg-salmon.jpg" alt="Salmon and egg rice in a glass bowl, topped with crisp smoky fish skin" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><span>05</span> The salmon and crisp skin made sense; egg mixed too early blurred the grains beneath them.</figcaption></figure>

<h2 id="nigiri">When the nigiri began, the dinner finally found its footing</h2>

Sushi Sho became markedly more assured once the nigiri sequence started.

The shari was made with red vinegar: distinct grains, clear acidity and enough firmness to demand chewing. A restrained suzuki came first, followed by a scallop from northern Norway.

This hotate had a different personality from the Hokkaido scallops I know. A strong Hokkaido specimen often moves from plumpness to a clean snap, releasing sweetness as the fibres part. The Norwegian scallop was softer and creamier. Against firm, assertive shari, that created an intriguing collision of textures, though not a perfectly balanced one. I still preferred the elasticity and clean sweetness of Hokkaido hotate.

<h2 id="shrimp">Nordic shrimp: the reason this restaurant should remain in Stockholm</h2>

Several small Nordic cold-water shrimp were assembled into a single piece of nigiri. Rather than the sticky softness of excellent amaebi, these shrimp had more bounce; their sweetness emerged through chewing.

For the first time, the topping and the rice met at equal strength. The shrimp had texture; so did the grains. Their clean sweetness could carry the fermented aroma and acidity of the shari. Most importantly, the chef stopped adding things—no horseradish drift, no extra citrus, no complicated sauce.

**The Nordic identity came from the ingredient itself.**

That was more persuasive than any dish loudly announcing “Nordic fusion”: a genuinely good local cold-water ingredient, expressed through Edomae rice and technique.

<h2 id="akami">Akami nigiri: lean tuna finally connects with the shari</h2>

A later piece of akami tasted notably cleaner. The unpleasant bloody acidity from the earlier dish was gone. Light seasoning did not obscure the fish, and the lean tuna finally connected naturally with the fermented depth of the rice.

<figure class="story-photo story-photo--portrait"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/11-akami-nigiri.jpg" alt="Akami tuna nigiri on a black ceramic slab" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><span>09</span> The same cut in a quieter form: no forceful condiment, and a far cleaner result.</figcaption></figure>

<h2 id="tomato">Three tomatoes: a reset between akami and aburi engawa</h2>

Immediately after the akami, and before the aburi engawa, the chef served what looked like a modest tomato course.

Three colours of tomato sat beneath a cool, translucent tomato jelly and fine threads of shiso. It was fresh and appetite-opening, but not merely acidic.

Its exact position mattered. The fermented depth of akami and red-vinegar rice had just receded; the heat and richer fat of torched engawa were about to arrive. Between them, the tomato jelly delivered a short, complete palate reset.

It performed a function similar to ginger at a sushi counter while speaking in a completely different vocabulary. This is the kind of fusion I trust: not local ingredients piled onto Japanese food, but an understanding of why a traditional element exists, followed by a local re-expression of that function.

<figure class="story-photo story-photo--portrait"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/11.5-tomato-overview.jpg" alt="Three colours of tomato with clear tomato jelly and fine shiso threads in a glass bowl" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><span>10</span> After akami, before aburi engawa: not an imitation of ginger, but the same reset expressed in a local language.</figcaption></figure>

<h2 id="engawa">Aburi engawa: after the reset, heat and fat take over</h2>

After that reset, the aburi engawa was confidently handled. A thick cut of flounder fin muscle was torched so its surface fat melted and caramelised while the centre retained its gelatinous pull, elasticity and fibres. A thin glaze added only a final note of sweet salinity.

The progression was complete: **char, warm fat, gelatinous resistance, the acid of the rice, then a faint sweetness from the glaze.**

The chef had finally stopped doing too much. That restraint was precisely why it worked.

<h2 id="toro">The best decision of the night: changing toro tartare to nigiri</h2>

The dinner’s most dramatic moment arrived with the toro.

The chef showed us a beautifully marbled section of tuna belly and offered three preparations: sashimi, nigiri, or tartare with caviar. I initially chose the tartare, reconsidered, and changed my order to nigiri.

<figure class="story-pair"><div><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/13-toro-raw.jpg" alt="A marbled section of tuna belly presented in a wooden tray" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><img src="../../../images/sushi-sho/16-tartare-lineup.jpg" alt="Pre-portioned tuna tartare topped with caviar on the counter" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></div><figcaption><span>12—13</span> The intact toro being cut in front of me and the pre-portioned tartare were two different propositions.</figcaption></figure>

The tartare in the kitchen had already been chopped and portioned in a container. A photograph alone cannot tell us which cut it came from, nor would it be fair to call it poor quality. What was certain, however, was that it was not the same proposition as watching the chef cut neta directly from the marbled piece in front of me.

Tartare crowned with caviar is almost designed to please: tuna fat, salt and umami. But chopping the fish compresses much of what made that particular piece valuable.

Whole toro first offers the resistance of muscle fibre. Body heat then begins to melt the interlaced fat, releasing sweetness and savouriness in stages. The marbling visible to the eye becomes texture in the mouth.

> Tartare offers a short route: soft tuna, popping caviar, finish. Nigiri allows fish, knife work, temperature, shari and seasoning to complete the bite together.

The two pieces that arrived were richly fatty but still recognisably meaty. Underneath, the acidic, distinct-grained shari finally found its ideal partner: the toro was soft, sweet and unctuous; the rice was warm, acidic and resistant. Each corrected the other’s excess.

<h2 id="verdict">The final judgment</h2>

Sushi Sho was not consistently convincing from beginning to end.

The opening exposed several weaknesses: dashi that did not persuade, a loose fish-ball texture, bloody acidity in tuna, rice blurred by too much egg, and an inclination to push acid too far.

Yet the chef’s foundations became clearer during the nigiri sequence. The red-vinegar rice had character, knife work grew more assured, and seasoning became restrained. The Nordic shrimp, later akami, the tomato reset placed between akami and aburi engawa, and the final toro were all worth remembering.

Measured against the highest Edomae counters in Tokyo, there is still distance. Placed back in Stockholm, however, the restaurant has genuine value.

That value does not come from caviar, elaborate sauces or the label of fusion. Its best moments arrived when the chef did less:

> **Let Nordic ingredients carry the place, Edomae technique hold the order, and the fish speak for itself.**
